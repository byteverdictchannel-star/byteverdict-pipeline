## How to Use This File

This file covers:
- The cost side — what producing a clip costs the venture (in time and any money)
- The revenue side — what a clip can earn, by route, under what eligibility constraints
- Per-clip economics — the unit economics of one clip, under stated assumptions
- Per-channel economics — the channel-level economics, under stated assumptions

All numbers are illustrative, under stated assumptions, with confidence levels. They are not predictions. They are internally consistent with the stated assumptions and are designed to surface the trade-offs, not to forecast revenue.

## Confidence Convention

- **High confidence:** Well-established structural fact (e.g., the channel has zero budget; the operator's time is the scarce capital; platform monetization is gated).
- **Medium confidence:** Established framework with fact-specific application (e.g., which costs are real, which revenue paths have which eligibility constraints, which assumptions dominate).
- **Low confidence:** Uncertain, evolving, or dependent on facts not yet known (e.g., the specific revenue per clip, the specific eligibility outcome, the specific audience/response the channel will get).

## 1. The Cost Side

### 1.1 Monetary cost — zero, by constraint

The venture is zero budget. Monetary cost is zero for:
- Sourcing (free footage, free platforms)
- Tooling (free tools)
- Editing (free tools)
- Distribution (free accounts)
- Off-platform ownership (cheap to start)

The zero-budget constraint means the channel cannot license sources, cannot buy tooling, cannot outsource, and cannot spend its way out of eligibility or risk problems. Monetary cost is effectively a non-factor at this stage — which means the real cost is time, and time is the scarce capital.

### 1.2 Time cost — the real cost

The real cost of a clip is the operator's time. The time sits in:
- **Sourcing and vetting** — finding, vetting, logging candidates.
- **Overlay writing** — writing specific, accurate, value-adding overlays.
- **Editing** — cutting, overlaying, exporting.
- **Screening** — sensitive-content checks.
- **Distribution** — re-purposing across the three surfaces, posting, monitoring.
- **Logging and review** — maintaining the source log, clip log, risk register.

The per-clip time is not known yet — it will be measured in the test batch. For modeling, the CFO uses an illustrative range and flags it as the key assumption to measure.

### 1.3 Implicit cost — opportunity cost of time

The operator's time has an opportunity cost. An hour spent on a clip is an hour not spent on something else — another clip, better sourcing, better overlays, the owned layer, learning the workflow, or anything else. The CFO's models should treat time as scarce capital with an opportunity cost, not as a free resource.

**The honest framing:** At zero budget, the venture's scarce capital is the operator's time and attention. Every monetization decision should be evaluated against the opportunity cost of the time it consumes.

## 2. The Revenue Side — By Route

### 2.1 YouTube Partner Program (YPP) revenue

**Structural reality:** YPP revenue is ad revenue from the channel's videos, including Shorts, once eligible. Revenue depends on views, engagement, advertiser-friendliness, and the platform's ad rates — all of which are platform-controlled and variable.

**For this channel, the key points:**
- Revenue is gated by eligibility. No eligibility, no revenue.
- Revenue is variable — per-view revenue varies by content, audience, geography, advertiser demand, and platform mechanics.
- Revenue is platform-controlled — YouTube controls the rates and the monetization.
- Revenue may be limited by the format's eligibility standing and by the content's advertiser-friendliness.

**Illustrative modeling point:** YPP revenue is a function of (a) eligibility (gated), (b) volume of views, (c) per-view revenue, and (d) advertiser-friendliness. The channel controls (b) partially through content and consistency, and (d) partially through genre and content choices — but (a) is the gating question and (c) is largely platform-controlled.

### 2.2 Off-platform ownership revenue

**Structural reality:** An owned audience layer can monetize through direct monetization, sponsorships, products, and audience data — but only once there's an audience to own.

**For this channel, the key points:**
- No revenue until there's an audience to own.
- Strategic value is immediate; revenue is later.
- Revenue potential depends on the audience the channel builds and the monetization layer chosen.
- Revenue is owned by the venture, not platform-controlled — a real difference from platform monetization.

### 2.3 TikTok Creator Programs revenue

**Structural reality:** TikTok monetization is gated by eligibility — account standing, originality/reused-content expectations, engagement thresholds, geographic/age requirements.

**For this channel, the key points:**
- Revenue is gated by eligibility.
- Revenue is variable and platform-controlled.
- Revenue is tied to reach, which is platform-controlled and variable.
- Sensitivity enforcement on war content can affect reach and eligibility.

### 2.4 Instagram monetization features revenue

**Structural reality:** Program-specific, gated, secondary surface.

**For this channel, the key points:**
- Revenue is secondary; the surface is secondary.
- Gated by program-specific eligibility.
- Not the primary focus.

### 2.5 Sponsorship / affiliate revenue

**Structural reality:** Sponsorship revenue requires an audience and a fit. Value comes from reach, engagement, and audience fit.

**For this channel, the key points:**
- No revenue until there's an audience.
- Revenue potential is broadened by the genre expansion to tech + viral.
- Revenue depends on the audience the channel builds and the sponsor fit.
- Brand alignment is a consideration, especially for war/news content.

### 2.6 Product revenue

**Structural reality:** A product requires an audience, a product thesis, and execution.

**For this channel, the key points:**
- No revenue at this stage.
- Requires audience first.
- Requires product thesis and execution.
- Keep the channel structured for it, but don't build a product strategy on a test batch.

## 4. Per-Channel Economics (Illustrative)

### 4.1 The channel as a time-investment business

At zero budget, the channel is a time-investment business. The operator invests time; the channel builds audience, eligibility trajectory, owned layer, and sponsor-readiness; monetization revenue comes later, if and when eligibility and audience materialize.

### 4.2 The key economic question

The key economic question for the channel is not "what is the revenue per clip?" — it's "is the time the operator invests in this channel likely to produce a return (in audience, eligibility, owned layer, sponsorship potential, or product potential) that exceeds the opportunity cost of that time?"

This is a strategic economic question, not a per-clip P&L question. It depends on:
- Whether the channel can build an audience.
- Whether the channel can reach monetization eligibility.
- Whether the channel can build an owned layer and sponsor-readiness.
- Whether the time invested is the best use of the operator's time relative to alternatives.

### 4.3 The break-even thinking (strategic, not per-clip)

The channel breaks even, strategically, when the monetization revenue (across routes) exceeds the time cost (with opportunity cost) of producing the channel — including the time spent building the owned layer, maintaining the workflow, and doing the research and planning.

At this stage, the break-even question is far out of reach — the channel has no revenue and is investing time. The break-even thinking is a later-stage question: once the channel has an audience and eligibility trajectory, does the monetization revenue justify the ongoing time cost?

### 4.4 The sensitivity around break-even

The break-even point is highly sensitive to:
- **Eligibility** — if the channel can't clear platform monetization eligibility, the revenue side is much lower.
- **Audience** — if the channel can't build an audience, the revenue side is much lower (across all routes).
- **Time cost per clip** — if the per-clip time is high, the channel needs more revenue to break even.
- **Genre/monetization fit** — if the audience fits sponsor categories well, sponsorship adds a revenue lever; if not, it doesn't.
- **Platform volatility** — if platforms change rules or enforcement, the revenue side can change.

## 5. The Assumptions That Matter Most

These are the assumptions that most affect the economics. Each is documented with a source and confidence level in L3 (`03-dossiers/04-unit-economics-assumptions.md`).

1. **Per-clip time (T):** The single most important operational assumption. Drives the cost side. To be measured in the test batch.
2. **Eligibility outcome (YouTube, TikTok, Instagram):** The gating assumption for platform revenue. Confidential uncertainty — depends on content pattern and evolving rules.
3. **Audience growth:** The assumption that drives revenue across all routes. Unknown at this stage.
4. **Per-view revenue (platform):** Largely platform-controlled and variable. Medium confidence on the structural reality; low confidence on the specific number for this channel.
5. **Sponsor fit:** The assumption that drives sponsorship revenue. Depends on the audience and genre mix the channel lands on.
6. **Opportunity cost of the operator's time:** The assumption that drives whether the time is well spent. The operator must supply this — it's a personal/strategic valuation, not a market price.
7. **Genre mix over time:** The assumption that drives advertiser-friendliness, sensitivity profile, and sponsor fit. The channel will evolve its mix; the assumption is about where it lands.

## 6. Sensitivity — What Breaks the Model

### If per-clip time is high

If producing a clip takes a lot of time, the channel needs more revenue (or more clips per unit time, or higher-value clips) to justify the time. High per-clip time is the most direct operational threat to the economics.

**Mitigation:** Better sourcing workflow, overlay templates, batch editing, subagent help where it fits, and eventually a sustainable cadence that fits the labor model.

### If eligibility is blocked

If the channel's format can't clear platform monetization eligibility, the platform revenue side is much lower. The channel would need to lean more on off-platform ownership and sponsorship (which have different eligibility dynamics) or evolve the format.

**Mitigation:** Build toward eligibility intentionally; be willing to evolve the format if eligibility becomes the binding constraint.

### If the audience doesn't build

If the channel doesn't build an audience, revenue across all routes is much lower. Audience building is the precondition for monetization.

**Mitigation:** Find the format and genre mix that gets traction; iterate on overlays and sourcing; be empirical about what works.

### If the operator's time is the wrong investment

If the time the operator invests in this channel is not the best use of that time relative to alternatives, the channel's economics are negative even if it eventually monetizes.

**Mitigation:** The operator should consciously value their time and decide whether this channel is the best use of it. The CFO's models can structure the question, but the answer is the operator's.

## 7. The CFO's Unit-Economics Bottom Line

1. **At zero budget, the real cost is the operator's time, with opportunity cost.** Monetary cost is effectively zero; time cost is the scarce capital.
2. **On a test batch, every clip is an investment of time with no revenue.** The question is whether the time is building the conditions for future monetization and whether it's well spent relative to alternatives.
3. **The per-clip economics are not yet knowable** — per-clip time must be measured, and per-clip revenue depends on eligibility, audience, and platform mechanics that are not yet established.
4. **The channel's economics are a strategic question, not a per-clip P&L.** The question is whether the time investment is likely to produce a return that exceeds the opportunity cost.
5. **The assumptions that matter most are:** per-clip time, eligibility outcome, audience growth, per-view revenue, sponsor fit, opportunity cost of time, and genre mix over time.
6. **The model is highly sensitive to eligibility, audience, per-clip time, and sponsor fit.** These are the variables that most affect whether the channel's economics work.
7. **The break-even question is a later-stage question.** Right now, the question is whether to invest time in building the channel — and whether that time is the best use of the operator's scarce capital.
