## 1. What Is Protected — Recap of Framework

*This dossier does not re-litigate the copyright framework; that is in `03-dossiers/dossier-copyright-fair-use.md`. The relevant point here is the licensing-landscape application:*

- War and news footage is presumptively copyrighted as a specific audiovisual work (the recording, editing, sequencing, graphics, audio).
- The underlying event is not copyrightable; the specific recording is.
- Clpping existing coverage is copying someone else's protected expression, not capturing the event directly.

## 2. Source Risk Tiers — Structured Landscape

*The tier ordering below is a structured landscape assessment, not a verbatim source. It is based on widely understood industry patterns: wire services and major broadcasters have licensing infrastructure and historically active enforcement; smaller creators and mixed-origin footage are more variable; certain government works may be public domain in narrow, source-specific cases.*

### Tier 1 — Highest practical risk: wire services and major broadcasters

- **Examples:** AP, Reuters, AFP, and similar wire services; major broadcast networks and their news divisions.
- **Characteristics:** Established entities with recognizable footage, branding, and often visible source markers; licensing infrastructure; historically active enforcement posture.
- **Why higher risk:** Their footage is widely reused and widely recognized, making unauthorized reuse easier to identify; they have licensing businesses that can be competed with or displaced by unauthorized reuse.

### Tier 2 — Moderate-to-high risk: established news outlets and sizable creators

- **Examples:** National and regional news outlets with their own video production; sizable creators whose footage is distinctive and widely reused.
- **Characteristics:** May have their own enforcement and licensing; distinctive footage is easier to identify and claim; platform-native claim flows may be used.

### Tier 3 — Variable risk: smaller creators, aggregators, and mixed-origin footage

- **Examples:** Smaller social creators; aggregator channels; footage whose origin is unclear or multi-hop.
- **Characteristics:** Enforcement may or may not be active; aggregator channels may be reusing footage they do not fully control; unclear origin is hard to assess and hard to clear.

### Tier 4 — Special case: potentially public-domain government footage

- **Examples:** Certain US federal government employee works as part of official duties (potentially public domain in the US under 17 U.S.C. § 105); other government entities with their own rules.
- **Characteristics:** Narrow and source-specific; not a blanket safe harbor; other governments and other US entities/contractors may hold rights; even where the underlying footage is public domain, an edited package may contain additional copyrighted elements (graphics, music, editing) from the presenter.

## 3. Filming vs. Clipping — The Channel's Actual Model

*The channel's model is clipping existing third-party footage, not filming the event. This is a copying model, not a capture model, and it normally requires a license for lawful wholesale reuse. The licensing landscape therefore concerns reusing others' recordings, not the possibly-cleaner question of footage the channel itself captured.*

If the channel later films anything directly, the channel would generally own the copyright in that recording (subject to contributor agreements). That would reduce the copyright-inference surface for those clips but would not eliminate all risk (other protected elements, platform sensitivity, sanctions/export considerations, jurisdictional issues).

## 4. Licensing Reality Under Zero Budget

*This is a structured assessment, not a verbatim source.*

- **Zero budget means no licensing clearance.** The channel cannot pay for licenses from wire services, broadcasters, agencies, or creators for the bulk of its content.
- **What zero budget does:** forces reliance on source selection, transformation arguments, and platform tolerance/enforcement vagaries.
- **What zero budget does not:** make infringement lawful, create a fair use right, remove platform enforcement risk, or remove the need to tier sources by risk.
- **The honest shape:** the channel's defensibility is a portfolio of partial measures and tolerances, not a single clean right-to-use. This does not mean the channel cannot operate; it means the channel operates on the contested side of the line for at least some clips.

## 5. Wire Service Footage — Practical Characteristics

*Wire service footage (AP, Reuters, AFP, etc.) is widely syndicated and recognized, often used by many outlets, which makes unauthorized reuse both easier to identify and more likely to be noticed by the wire. These organizations have historically treated such footage as part of their licensing business. The practical implication is that clips sourced from wire services carry the highest practical enforcement and licensing risk, and the channel should treat them as requiring the most caution — and, under a zero-budget model, as the sources most likely to be avoided or used only in the most transformative, minimal, and carefully contextualized way, if at all.*

---

## 6. Broadcast Footage — Practical Characteristics

*Broadcast news footage (network packages, live hits, field reports) is recognizable and branded in many cases, produced by entities with their own enforcement and licensing posture, and often the subject of reuse by other outlets, which increases the chance of detection. The practical implication is that broadcast footage sits in a similar high-to-moderate-high risk band, depending on the specific outlet and the recognizability of the package.*

---

## 7. Chain-of-Rights Problem for Aggregator and Multi-Hop Footage

- When the channel clips from another creator or aggregator who is themselves clipping from a wire or broadcaster, the channel is two hops from the original rights holder.
- The immediate source may or may not have the right to redistribute; the channel's use may still ultimately implicate the original rightsholder.
- "A creator posted it" is not a clean clearance; the creator may have clipped it from a wire, may have a license, or may not. Platform hosting does not grant downstream users a copyright license.
- Multi-hop sourcing does not reduce the underlying rights question; it can add ambiguity and chain-of-rights problems.

---

## 8. Embedding / Sharing Tools vs. General Licensing

- Some sources provide embed codes, share features, or platform-specific redistribution tools. These can authorize specific forms of sharing through specific mechanisms, but they are not a blanket license to download, re-edit, re-cut, and republish the footage on other platforms with overlays.
- Platform Terms of Service for the source do not, as a general matter, grant a downstream user the right to reuse the copyrighted content in a new compilation with overlays on other platforms. The channel's model — cut + text overlay + republish to TikTok/YouTube/Instagram — is downstream reuse, not mere sharing-through-the-source's-mechanism.

## 9. Confidence and Uncertainty

| Statement | Confidence |
|-----------|-----------|
| Wire services and major broadcasters are the highest practical enforcement/licensing risk tier | High (tier ordering); Medium (source-specific enforcement posture) |
| Smaller creators and unclear-origin footage are variable and harder to assess | High |
| Certain US federal government works may be public domain in the US — but narrow and source-specific | Medium–High (legal rule); Medium (application to specific footage) |
| "A creator posted it" is not a clean clearance | High |
| Embed/share tools are not a blanket license for downstream cut+overlay+republish | High |
| Zero budget means no licensing clearance, which forces reliance on source selection, transformation arguments, and platform tolerance | High |

### Key uncertainties

1. Source-specific enforcement posture — general tier ordering is stable; specific posture is not perfectly knowable.
2. Ownership chains for freelance/agency material — can be murky.
3. Public-domain scope for government footage — real but narrow; requires source-specific verification.
4. International overlay — US-centric context; other jurisdictions may apply depending on audience and monetization.
