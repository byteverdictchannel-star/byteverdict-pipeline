## Statutory Framework (verbatim from U.S. Copyright Office)

*Source: U.S. Copyright Office — "About Fair Use," https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/. Retrieved 2026-08-24.*

### 1. The statute

> "Section 107 of the Copyright Act provides the statutory framework for determining whether something is a fair use and identifies certain types of uses—such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research—as examples of activities that may qualify as fair use. Section 107 calls for consideration of the following four factors in evaluating a question of fair use:"

### 2. The four factors (verbatim)

> "1. Purpose and character of the use, including whether the use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes: Courts look at how the party claiming fair use is using the copyrighted work, and are more likely to find that nonprofit educational and noncommercial uses are fair. This does not mean, however, that all nonprofit education and noncommercial uses are fair and all commercial uses are not fair; instead, courts will balance the purpose and character of the use against the other factors below. Additionally, 'transformative' uses are more likely to be considered fair. Transformative uses are those that add something new, with a further purpose or different character, and do not substitute for the original use of the work."

> "2. Nature of the copyrighted work: This factor analyzes the degree to which the work that was used relates to copyright's purpose of encouraging creative expression. Thus, using a more creative or imaginative work (such as a novel, movie, or song) is less likely to support a claim of a fair use than using a factual work (such as a technical article or news item). In addition, use of an unpublished work is less likely to be considered fair."

> "3. Amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole: Under this factor, courts look at both the quantity and quality of the copyrighted material that was used. If the use includes a large portion of the copyrighted work, fair use is less likely to be found; if the use employs only a small amount of copyrighted material, fair use is more likely. That said, some courts have found use of an entire work to be fair under certain circumstances. And in other contexts, using even a small amount of a copyrighted work was determined not to be fair because the selection was an important part—or the 'heart'—of the work."

> "4. Effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work: Here, courts review whether, and to what extent, the unlicensed use harms the existing or future market for the copyright owner's original work. In assessing this factor, courts consider whether the use is hurting the current market for the original work (for example, by displacing sales of the original) and/or whether the use could cause substantial harm if it were to become widespread."

### 3. Case-specific nature (verbatim)

> "In addition to the above, other factors may also be considered by a court in weighing a fair use question, depending upon the circumstances. Courts evaluate fair use claims on a case-by-case basis, and the outcome of any given case depends on a fact-specific inquiry. This means that there is no formula to ensure that a predetermined percentage or amount of a work—or specific number of words, lines, pages, copies—may be used without permission."

### 4. Disclaimer (verbatim)

> "Although the Fair Use Index should prove helpful in understanding what courts have to date considered to be fair or not fair, it is not a substitute for legal advice."

> "Please note that the Copyright Office is unable to provide specific legal advice to individual members of the public about questions of fair use."

## Encyclopedic Overview — Key Points (paraphrased/structured from Wikipedia)

*Source: Wikipedia — "Fair use," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_use. Last reviewed Aug 13, 2026. Retrieved 2026-08-24.*

### 1. Definition and status

- Fair use is a doctrine in US law that permits limited use of copyrighted material without permission from the copyright holder.
- It is a limitation to copyright intended to balance the interests of copyright holders with the public interest in wider distribution and use of creative works.
- It is codified in 17 U.S.C. § 107 (Copyright Act of 1976).
- The Supreme Court has issued several major decisions clarifying and reaffirming fair use since the 1980s; the most recent major decision cited is *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* (2023).

### 2. Fair use as an affirmative defense / exception

- The Supreme Court has traditionally characterized fair use as an affirmative defense.
- In *Lenz v. Universal Music Corp.* (2015) (the "dancing baby" case), the Ninth Circuit concluded that fair use was "not merely a defense to an infringement claim, but was an expressly authorized right, and an exception to the exclusive rights granted to the author of a creative work."
- Quote from § 107: "the fair use of a copyrighted work... is not an infringement of copyright."

### 3. Examples of fair use

- Commentary, criticism, news reporting, research, scholarship, parody, and search engines are commonly cited examples.

### 4. Key doctrinal points relevant to the channel

- **Transformative use** — uses that add something new, with a further purpose or different character, and do not substitute for the original — are more likely to be fair. This is the central concept for the channel's overlay-based transformation argument.
- **Nature of the work** — factual works (such as a news item) get thinner protection than highly creative works; this is one reason news footage can be a marginally better fair use candidate than fiction — but it is not a get-out-of-jail-free card, because the specific recording is still creative expression.
- **Amount and substantiality** — both quantitative and qualitative; the "heart of the work" can be problematic even in small amounts.
- **Market effect** — the most heavily weighted factor in many disputes; includes both actual and potential market harm, and whether the use would cause substantial harm if widespread.
- **No bright-line rules** — "there is no formula to ensure that a predetermined percentage or amount of a work... may be used without permission." This is the structural basis for the analysis's point that the channel's specific format is not cleanly resolved by existing precedent.

### 5. Related case-law themes (not verbatim; background context)

- *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* (1994) — Supreme Court decision on parody and transformative use; established that commercial nature does not automatically defeat fair use and that transformative use is central.
- *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith* (2023) — Supreme Court decision on the scope of transformative use; the Court held that a closely related secondary work that shares the same commercial purpose as the original may not be sufficiently transformative, even with stylistic differences. This is relevant context for the analysis's point that "transformative" is not a magic word and that closely related commercial uses can be problematic.

## Why These Notes Matter for the Channel

1. **The four-factor framework is the actual legal lens.** Any fair use argument the channel might make would be evaluated under these four factors, not under a "text overlay = fair use" rule.
2. **Transformative use is central but not unlimited.** The Copyright Office definition and the *Warhol* decision both make clear that "transformative" is not infinite — closely related commercial uses can fail the test even with modifications.
3. **"News reporting" is listed as an example purpose, but that does not mean all news-adjacent clipping is fair.** Section 107 lists examples of activities that *may* qualify; it does not guarantee that a given news-adjacent use qualifies.
4. **News footage is factual but the recording is creative.** Factor 2 can slightly favor fair use for factual works, but the specific audiovisual recording is still creative expression. This nuance is the basis for the analysis's point that Factor 2 does not do much heavy lifting either way.
5. **Case-by-case, fact-specific.** There is no formula. This is the core reason the channel cannot get a general "this is fair use" conclusion at the landscape level — it's clip-by-clip.
6. **The Copyright Office itself disclaims legal advice.** Even the official resource says it's not a substitute for legal advice. This reinforces the report's stance that the landscape is not a clearance.
