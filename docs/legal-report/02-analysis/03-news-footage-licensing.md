# News Footage Licensing Reality

**Thesis:** War and news footage is protected by copyright, and the "sourcing model" of clipping existing third-party footage is, by design, a copying model that normally requires a license for lawful wholesale reuse. The relevant practical questions are: which sources carry the highest enforcement and licensing risk, what is the difference between footage the channel itself captured and footage clipped from others, and what does "zero budget" mean for the licensing landscape — namely, that the channel cannot clear rights through licensing and must instead rely on source selection, transformation arguments, and platform tolerance, with the honest understanding that these modulate but do not eliminate risk.

**This is a landscape and risk assessment. It is NOT legal advice.**

---

## 1. What Is Protected

### 1.1 The specific recording, not the event

The copyrightable object is the specific audiovisual work: the particular camera work, framing, editing, sequencing, graphics, audio, and packaging produced by the broadcaster, wire service, or creator. The underlying event — the battle, the protest, the announcement, the disaster — is not itself copyrightable.

This is the same point made in the copyright analysis (`02-analysis/01-copyright-fair-use.md`), repeated here because it is the foundation for the licensing landscape: **you are licensing (or infringing) the recording, not the event.**

### 1.2 Who holds the rights

Rights can sit with:
- **Broadcasters** — for their news packages and live coverage.
- **Wire services** — for their agency footage and photo/video packages.
- **Freelance camerapersons / journalists** — depending on contract terms; often contractually assigned to the commissioning outlet, but not always trivially clear.
- **Social creators** — for footage they recorded and published.
- **Agencies / photo agencies** — for still imagery and, in some cases, video.
- **Government entities** — in some cases, with jurisdiction-specific and entity-specific caveats (see Section 4).

The ownership chain is not always clean on its face, especially for freelance and agency material, but the practical default is that the person or entity that produced and published the footage has some form of control over its reuse.

### 1.3 Footage can be licensed, claimed, or simply unenforced

A given clip may be:
- **Actively licensed and enforced** — the rights holder has a licensing business and enforces reuse.
- **Available under some license** — e.g., the source has licensing terms, embed codes, or platform-specific sharing rules.
- **Claimed via platform systems** — e.g., Content ID or similar, redirecting revenue or blocking reuse.
- **Not actively enforced** — the rights holder is not pursuing reuse, at least for now.

The channel's risk is a function not just of what is protected, but of which sources are in which category.

## 2. Source Risk Tiers

These tiers are about practical enforcement and licensing risk, not about whether the footage is copyrighted (it almost always is).

### 2.1 Tier 1 — Highest risk: wire services and major broadcasters

**Examples:** AP, Reuters, AFP, and similar wire services; major broadcast networks and their news divisions.

**Why higher risk:**
- These are established entities with recognizable footage, branding, and often visible source markers.
- They have licensing infrastructure and have historically been active in licensing and enforcement.
- Their footage is widely reused and widely recognized, which makes unauthorized reuse easier to identify.
- They are the kind of rights holders most likely to use takedown/claim systems and to pursue reuse that competes with their licensing market.

**Practical implication:** Clips sourced from wire services and major broadcasters carry the highest practical enforcement and licensing risk. The channel should treat these as requiring the most caution — and, under a zero-budget model, as the sources most likely to be avoided or used only in the most transformative, minimal, and carefully contextualized way, if at all.

### 2.2 Tier 2 — Moderate-to-high risk: established news outlets and sizable creators

**Examples:** National and regional news outlets with their own video production; sizable creators whose footage is distinctive and widely reused.

**Why moderate-to-high risk:**
- Established outlets may have their own enforcement and licensing.
- Sizable creators may enforce through platform claim flows, direct takedown requests, or community norms.
- Footage that is distinctive and widely reused is easier to identify and claim.

**Practical implication:** These sources are in the middle of the practical risk band. They are not automatically "safe," and they are not automatically "doomed." The channel's handling — portion used, overlays added, context provided, whether the source is recognizable and likely to care — drives where on the band any given clip lands.

### 2.3 Tier 3 — Variable risk: smaller creators, aggregators, and mixed-origin footage

**Examples:** Smaller social creators; aggregator channels; footage whose origin is unclear or multi-hop.

**Why variable risk:**
- Smaller creators may or may not enforce; some do, some do not.
- Aggregator channels may be reusing footage they do not fully control, which can create chain-of-rights problems.
- Footage with unclear origin is hard to license even if you wanted to, and hard to assess for enforcement risk.

**Practical implication:** "Unclear origin" is itself a risk signal. Footage whose source you cannot identify is hard to assess and hard to clear. The channel should generally prefer identifiable sources over unidentified ones, not because unidentified sources are necessarily lower risk, but because they are harder to evaluate and harder to defend against in a dispute.

### 2.4 Tier 4 — Special case: potentially public-domain government footage

**Examples:** Certain footage produced by US federal government employees as part of their official duties (which may be public domain in the US under 17 U.S.C. § 105); and footage from other government entities with their own rules.

**Why special and limited:**
- US federal government works are not subject to copyright in the US under § 105, but this is **entity-specific and jurisdiction-specific**, not a blanket rule for all "government footage."
- Other governments (and other US government entities, and contractors) may hold copyright or other rights.
- Even where the underlying footage is public domain, a particular edited package or broadcast presentation may contain additional copyrighted elements (graphics, music, editing) from the presenter.
- Public domain in the US does not necessarily resolve all international questions if the audience or monetization touches other jurisdictions.

**Practical implication:** Public-domain government footage is a real, if narrow, category that can be lower-risk in specific, well-documented cases — but it is not a blanket safe harbor, and it requires source-specific verification. It should not be assumed by category label alone.

## 3. Filming vs. Clipping — The Channel's Actual Model

### 3.1 The channel's model is clipping, not filming

The brief specifies existing third-party footage. That is the model. The licensing landscape therefore concerns **reusing others' recordings**, not the possibly-cleaner question of footage the channel itself captured.

### 3.2 If the channel later films anything, the calculus changes — but only partially

If the channel (or a contributor) filmed footage directly, the channel would generally own the copyright in that recording (subject to any contributor agreements). That would remove the "clipping others' footage" problem for that footage. But it would not eliminate all legal risk: war footage can still involve other protected elements (incidental inclusion of third-party footage, graphics, or branding; jurisdictional issues; platform sensitivity rules; and sanctions/export considerations for certain content).

**Practical implication:** Moving toward original capture is a way to reduce the copyright-inference surface for some clips, but it is not a complete risk eliminator, and it has its own practical and safety constraints (especially for war content, where direct capture raises serious safety, access, and ethical questions beyond the scope of this report).

## 4. The Licensing Reality Under Zero Budget

### 4.1 Zero budget means no licensing clearance

With zero budget, the channel cannot pay for licenses. That is the definitional constraint. The consequence is that the channel cannot, as a general matter, clear rights through licensing for the bulk of its content. This is not a moral judgment; it is the operational reality.

### 4.2 What zero budget does and does not do

**It does:**
- Force the channel to rely on (a) source selection that reduces exposure, (b) transformation arguments for the clips it does use, and (c) platform tolerance and enforcement vagaries.
- Make the channel operate on the contested side of the line for at least some clips, with no licensing safety net.

**It does not:**
- Make infringement lawful.
- Make fair use "free" or risk-free (fair use is an argument, not a clearance).
- Remove platform enforcement risk.
- Remove the need to think about which sources are higher-risk and which are lower-risk.

### 4.3 The honest shape of operating without a licensing net

The honest framing is: a zero-budget clips channel built on third-party footage is, by design, a channel that relies on arguments, tolerances, and source-selection heuristics rather than on clearances. That is a coherent model for some creators at some scale, but it is not the same as a cleared, licensed model, and it does not convert to one without either (a) budget for licensing, (b) a shift toward original capture, or (c) acceptance that some clips carry unresolved risk.

This is the key "zero-budget constraint" finding: the absence of licensing money does not mean the channel cannot operate at all; it means the channel's defensibility is a portfolio of partial measures rather than a single clean right-to-use.

## 5. What "Wire Service Footage" Actually Means in Practice

### 5.1 Wire services are a distinct risk concentration

Wire service footage (AP, Reuters, AFP, etc.) is:
- Widely syndicated and recognized.
- Often used by many outlets, which makes unauthorized reuse both easier to identify and more likely to be noticed by the wire.
- A class of material that these organizations have historically treated as part of their licensing business.

**Practical implication:** If the channel's sourcing leans heavily on wire service footage, it is concentrating risk in the highest-risk tier. If the channel wants to reduce that concentration, it should reduce reliance on wire service footage specifically — or use it only in the most carefully contextualized, minimal, and transformative ways, understanding that even then the underlying risk tier is higher.

### 5.2 Broadcast footage has similar characteristics

Broadcast news footage (network packages, live hits, field reports) is:
- Recognizable and branded in many cases.
- Produced by entities with their own enforcement and licensing posture.
- Often the subject of reuse by other outlets, which increases the chance of detection.

**Practical implication:** Broadcast footage sits in a similar high-to-moderate-high risk band, depending on the specific outlet and the recognizability of the package.

## 6. The Chain-of-Rights Problem for Aggregator and Multi-Hop Footage

### 6.1 Aggregator footage can be second-hand

When the channel clips from another creator or aggregator who is themselves clipping from a wire or broadcaster, the channel is now two hops from the original rights holder. The immediate source may or may not have the right to redistribute, and the channel's use may still ultimately implicate the original rightsholder.

### 6.2 "A creator posted it" is not a clean clearance

The fact that a creator posted footage on a platform does not resolve the underlying rights. The creator may have clipped it from a wire; the creator may have a license; the creator may not. The platform's hosting of the footage is also not a license to downstream users (platform ToS generally do not grant downstream users a copyright license in the content others post).

**Practical implication:** Multi-hop sourcing does not reduce the underlying rights question; it can add ambiguity and chain-of-rights problems. The channel should treat "I found it on a creator's post" as a sourcing observation, not as a clearance.

---

## 7. Licensing vs. Platform Embedding / Sharing Tools

### 7.1 Embed codes and platform share features are not general licenses

Some sources provide embed codes, share features, or platform-specific redistribution tools. These can authorize specific forms of sharing through specific mechanisms, but they are not a blanket license to download, re-edit, re-cut, and republish the footage on other platforms with overlays.

### 7.2 Platform Terms of Service for the source do not grant downstream rights

Even where a platform's terms allow hosting and viewing, those terms do not, as a general matter, grant a downstream user the right to reuse the copyrighted content in a new compilation with overlays on other platforms. The channel's model — cut + text overlay + republish to TikTok/YouTube/Instagram — is downstream reuse, not mere sharing-through-the-source's-mechanism.

## 8. Confidence Levels

| Statement | Confidence |
|-----------|-----------|
| War and news footage is presumptively copyrighted; the recording, not the event, is protected | High |
| Wire services and major broadcasters are the highest practical enforcement/licensing risk tier | High (tier ordering); Medium (source-specific enforcement posture) |
| Smaller creators and unclear-origin footage are variable and harder to assess | High |
| Certain US federal government works may be public domain in the US — but this is narrow and source-specific | Medium–High (the legal rule); Medium (application to specific footage) |
| "A creator posted it" is not a clean clearance | High |
| Embed/share tools are not a blanket license for downstream cut+overlay+republish | High |
| Zero budget means no licensing clearance, which forces reliance on source selection, transformation arguments, and platform tolerance | High |
| Moving toward original capture reduces the copyright-inference surface for those clips but does not eliminate all risk | Medium–High |

## 9. Key Uncertainties

1. **Source-specific enforcement posture.** Some sources enforce actively; some do not; some use platform claim systems. The general tier ordering is reasonably stable; the specific posture of any given source at any given time is not perfectly knowable without direct knowledge.
2. **Ownership chains for freelance and agency material.** These can be murky, which affects both licensing availability and enforcement likelihood.
3. **Public-domain scope for government footage.** This is real but narrow and requires source-specific verification; it should not be assumed.
4. **International overlay.** Where the audience or monetization touches jurisdictions beyond the US, additional considerations can arise. The brief centers US copyright and US-platform context; the international dimension is a real but secondary uncertainty.
