# Copyright & Fair Use Landscape (US)

**Thesis:** Third-party war and news footage is presumptively copyrighted. A clips channel that cuts and re-publishes that footage with text overlays is engaging in a use that *may* have a fair use argument — especially if the overlays add commentary, criticism, or substantial context — but text-overlay-only use of wholesale clips is not a clearly safe harbor. The four-factor analysis is fact-specific, unsettled at the exact resolution and format the channel operates at, and tilted against the channel in the most important factors when the source is used substantially and the overlays are thin.

**This is a landscape and risk assessment. It is NOT legal advice.**

---

## 1. The Core Legal Frame

### 1.1 Copyright attaches on fixation

Under US law, copyright protects original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium of expression. A news broadcast, a wire service video package, a photographer's or videographer's recording, and a social creator's clip are each presumptively protected the moment they are fixed. The fact that the underlying subject matter is newsworthy or factual does not strip the *specific expression* — the camera work, editing, sequencing, graphics, audio selection, timecodes, and packaging — of protection.

This is the single most important conceptual point for the channel: **clipping existing coverage is copying someone else's copyrighted expression**, even if the underlying event is public and even if a different recording of the same event (e.g., one the channel itself filmed) would raise entirely different questions.

### 1.2 "The event is public" is not a copyright defense

A war zone, a protest, a political event, a disaster — these are facts and events, not copyrightable. But the specific audiovisual recording of them is. Two different camera operators filming the same event produce two different copyrighted works. Using one operator's recording does not become lawful because the event is public; it becomes a question of whether the use of *that recording* is licensed, excused by fair use, or otherwise permitted.

### 1.3 Filming the event yourself vs. clipping existing coverage — different acts

If the channel (or a hired contributor) filmed the footage itself, the channel would own (or control) the copyright in that recording, and the question would be whether the event depicted involves other protected elements (e.g., a broadcaster's graphics, a third party's incidental inclusion). Clipping existing coverage is a different act: the channel is using someone else's fixed expression.

This distinction matters because the brief frames the sourcing model as "existing third-party footage — broadcast news clips, agency wire photos/video, other creators' clips." That model is, by design, a copying model, not a capture model.

## 2. Fair Use — The Four-Factor Landscape

### 2.1 The framework

Fair use is an affirmative defense under 17 U.S.C. § 107, evaluated case by case under four statutory factors:

1. **Purpose and character of the use** — including whether it is commercial and whether it is "transformative" (adds something new, with a different purpose or character, rather than merely superseding the original).
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work** — factual works get thinner protection than highly creative works; unpublished vs. published status can matter.
3. **Amount and substantiality of the portion used** — both quantitative (how much) and qualitative (the "heart" of the work).
4. **Effect of the use upon the potential market** — including actual and potential market harm, and whether the use serves a different market function.

No single factor is dispositive. Courts weigh them together. The channel's clips will be evaluated under this framework if a dispute arises.

### 2.2 Factor 1 — Purpose and character (where "text overlay" lives)

This is where the channel's transformation narrative lives. Adding headlines, captions, context lines, timestamps, and other text overlays is a form of modification. The question is whether that modification is "transformative" in the sense the courts have used that term: does it add new expression, meaning, or message, or does it primarily serve the same purpose as the source (showing the event) with some extra text on top?

Relevant considerations:
- **Commentary / criticism / reporting on the clip itself** is the classic strong transformative use. A clip used as the object of commentary, analysis, or criticism has a stronger Factor 1 than a clip used primarily to show the event.
- **Text overlays that add context** (who, what, when, why, what happened next) are more transformative than overlays that merely restate or sensationalize what is already visible.
- **Text overlays that are thin** — a headline and a timestamp over a clip that is otherwise presented as the raw event — are closer to "the same purpose, with extra words," which is a weaker transformative claim.
- **Commercial nature** cuts against fair use in the purpose analysis, though it is not fatal on its own. The channel's monetization intent is relevant here.

Confidence on the qualitative landscape: **High** — these are well-established qualitative principles. Confidence on how a given clip's specific overlays would score: **Medium to Low**, because that is inherently clip-by-clip and fact-specific, and because the case law on exactly this kind of short-form social overlay use is not cleanly settled at the channel's resolution and format.

### 2.3 Factor 2 — Nature of the copyrighted work

News footage is a mix:
- **Factual / newsworthy content** leans slightly toward fair use (factual works get thinner protection than highly creative works).
- But news footage is still *creative expression* in its specific recording, editing, and packaging — it is not a bare fact.

This factor generally does not do much heavy lifting either way in this context. It is not a strong shield for the channel and not a strong weapon against it.

Confidence: **Medium.** The factor exists and has the general directionality described, but it is rarely the decider in clip-reuse cases.

### 2.4 Factor 3 — Amount and substantiality (the weak point for wholesale clipping)

This factor asks how much of the source is used and whether what is taken is the "heart" of the work.

For the channel's model:
- If a clip is a **short source clip** (e.g., a 30-second news package) and the channel uses a large portion of it, even with overlays, this factor can weigh against fair use — especially if the portion taken is the most expressive or impactful part.
- If a clip is a **longer source** and the channel uses a short segment, this factor is more favorable — but only if the segment taken is also the segment *necessary* for the new purpose. Taking the "best" or "most viral" 15 seconds of a longer piece can still be "the heart" even if it is quantitatively small.
- **Montage / compilation** formats that string together many short clips from many sources can, in aggregate, take a substantial amount across many works.

This is the factor most directly in tension with the channel's "cut + text overlay" format when the cut is a substantial portion of a short source clip.

Confidence: **Medium.** The directional logic is clear; the application to any given clip is fact-specific.

### 2.5 Factor 4 — Market effect (the other weak point)

This factor asks whether the use harms the actual or potential market for the original, including licensing markets.

For the channel's model:
- **News footage licensing is a real market.** Wire services, broadcasters, and some creators license footage, and some monetize through syndication, licensing, and platform revenue. A channel that repurposes that footage on high-reach platforms can, in principle, compete with or substitute for that licensing market — particularly if the clip is presented as a substitute viewing experience.
- **The "different market" argument** (the channel is doing commentary/aggregation, not competing with the source's licensing market) is stronger when the overlays add substantial new value and the clip is not a drop-in replacement for the source. It is weaker when the clip is largely the raw footage with a few words on top.
- **Monetization intent** makes this factor more salient, because it moves the channel closer to a commercial substitute question.

This factor is highly fact-specific and often heavily weighted in clip-reuse disputes. It is not a clear "safe" or "doomed" factor for the channel generically; it depends on the specific clip, the specific source's markets, and the specific way the clip is presented.

Confidence: **Medium.** The framework is well-established; the application is fact-specific and the channel's exact market overlap with any given source is underdetermined by the generic model.

## 3. The "Transformative Use" Doctrine and Where the Channel Sits

### 3.1 Transformative use is not a magic word

"Transformative use" is a key concept in modern fair use analysis, especially under Factor 1, but it is not a standalone rule and not a license to do whatever modification you want. The central question is whether the new work adds something new with a different purpose or character — different aesthetic, different informational function, different audience — rather than merely repackaging the original.

### 3.2 What text overlays actually buy

Text overlays can be transformative when they:
- Add commentary, analysis, or criticism of the clip or the event.
- Provide substantial context that changes the informational purpose (e.g., explaining what is happening, why it matters, what happened before/after, correcting a misperception).
- Repackage the clip into a different functional product (e.g., a curated, annotated, contextual news summary).

Text overlays are *less* transformative when they:
- Primarily sensationalize or restate what is already visible.
- Add a headline and a timestamp but leave the clip otherwise as a raw viewing experience.
- Are thin enough that the clip remains the primary value and the overlays are secondary decoration.

The channel's test batch format — text overlays only, no voiceover, no on-camera presence — is, by design, operating on the thinner end of this spectrum. That does not make it infringement as a matter of law; it means the transformative-use argument is thinner than it would be with commentary, reaction, or substantial analysis.

### 3.3 The "compilation / aggregation" angle

If the channel's value proposition is aggregation and curation — "here are the key clips from this conflict, with context" — that is a different framing than "here is a clip, with a catchy headline." Aggregation and curation can have stronger fair use arguments when the value is the selection, organization, and contextualization, and when individual clips are used in a way that supports that larger purpose rather than standing alone as the primary viewing experience.

This is one of the more plausible stronger-use framings for the channel, but it depends on execution: the overlays and surrounding context must actually do the curatorial/contextual work, not just decorate individual clips.

## 4. The Attribution Question (Preview)

Attribution — "crediting the source" — is discussed in detail in `02-analysis/05-attribution-myth.md`. The short version for the fair use landscape: attribution is not one of the four factors, it is not a substitute for a license, and it does not automatically make a use fair. It can be a factual good-faith element in some courts' reasoning and can matter in platform dispute flows, but it is not a legal safety net. Do not rely on it as one.

## 5. What the Law Does and Does Not Settle for This Channel

### 5.1 What is relatively settled

- Third-party footage is presumptively copyrighted.
- "The event is public" is not a defense to copying someone's recording.
- Attribution is not a substitute for a license or a fair use finding.
- Fair use is a case-by-case, four-factor, affirmative defense — not a clear rule you can apply mechanically.

### 5.2 What is not settled — and is the real live uncertainty

- Exactly how a short-form social clip with text overlays, no voiceover, and monetization intent would fare under the four factors when sourced from a given wire service, broadcaster, or creator — **this is clip-by-clip, fact-specific, and not cleanly resolved by existing precedent at the channel's exact format.**
- Where the line falls between "transformative enough to be a plausible fair use" and "thin overlay on a wholesale clip that is more likely infringement" — this line is fuzzy and fact-dependent.
- How platform enforcement, takedown flows, and Content ID-style systems will interact with any underlying legal analysis — because a clip can be legally defensible in some sense and still get removed or claimed by a platform system.

This means the honest posture is: **the legal landscape supports a plausible fair use argument in some configurations, but it does not give the channel a general green light, and the strongest arguments depend on the channel actually doing the more transformative things (commentary, context, curation) rather than the thinner end of text-overlay-only use.**

## 7. Key Uncertainties

1. **Clip-by-clip variability.** Fair use is fact-specific. A channel-level "is this model fair use?" question is not really the right question; the right question is clip-by-clip, and the answer will vary.
2. **The thin-overlay vs. commentary spectrum.** The channel's current test-batch format sits on the thinner end. The legal landscape is more favorable to the more transformative configurations. How the channel chooses to position itself on that spectrum is a real decision with real legal implications.
3. **Platform enforcement as a parallel system.** A clip can be legally arguable and still be removed, claimed, or demonetized by platform systems that do not make fine legal distinctions. The legal landscape and the platform landscape are related but not identical.
4. **Enforcement posture of specific sources.** Some sources actively enforce; some do not; some use platform-native claim systems; some license. The legal analysis for a given clip is partly independent of, and partly entangled with, the source's actual enforcement behavior.
