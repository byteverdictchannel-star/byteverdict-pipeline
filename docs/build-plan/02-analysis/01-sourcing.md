# Sourcing Workflow — Zero Budget

**Where war/news clips come from when you have no money to license anything.** This is the front door of the channel. If sourcing is unreliable, nothing downstream matters.

> Companion: **Legal / Risk Landscape Report** (`~/clips-channel/legal-report/00-index.md`) — read its "News footage licensing reality" and "Risk-mitigation patterns" sections alongside this document.

---

## 1. The Core Constraint

Zero budget means **zero licensing money**. Every source in this document is free to access and free to clip — but "free to access" does not mean "free to republish." The legal/licensing reality lives in the Legal Report. This document is the operational sourcing workflow; it flags risk tiers but does not resolve legal questions.

The practical upshot: sourcing is about finding footage that is (a) genuinely free to access, (b) high enough quality/engagement to be worth clipping, and (c) positioned such that your overlay work gives you a plausible transformation argument. The sourcing workflow is also your first risk-control surface: **what you choose to clip and how much of it you use is a build decision that affects risk.**

## 2. Source Risk Tiers

Not all free footage is equal. Rank every candidate source into a tier before clipping. The tier drives how much footage you take, how you frame it, and what overlays you add.

### Tier 1 — Lowest Risk (Relatively)

**Public domain / government footage where the jurisdiction actually puts it in the public domain.**

- US federal government works (generally public domain in the US — but verify the specific agency and specific content; not all government footage is PD, and this is US-status-dependent).
- Some officially released military/public-affairs footage that is explicitly released for public use.
- Footage where the uploader is the rights-holder and has clearly licensed it permissively (e.g., Creative Commons with appropriate terms — check the specific CC variant; some CC licenses are incompatible with commercial use, some require share-alike).

**What Tier 1 buys:** A stronger starting position for transformation arguments, because you're building on footage with a cleaner rights status. Still not "safe" — your overlays, clip length, and context still matter.

**What Tier 1 doesn't buy:** Immunity from platform enforcement, trademark issues, sensitive-content flags, or the fact that a government source in one jurisdiction may not be PD in another.

### Tier 2 — Moderate Risk

**News footage from outlets where fair-use arguments are most commonly invoked, but where the source is a commercial rights-holder.**

- Major broadcast news clips (CNN, Fox, MSNBC, BBC, etc.) — widely clipped by third parties, but copyrighted by the broadcaster. This is the category where "text overlay" transformation arguments are most commonly made and most commonly contested.
- Wire service footage (AP, Reuters) — high quality, high engagement, and historically aggressive on licensing. This is one of the riskier Tier 2 sub-categories; wire services actively enforce.

**What Tier 2 buys:** Availability and engagement. This is where most trending war/news clips come from.

**What Tier 2 doesn't buy:** Comfort. The Legal Report's "News footage licensing reality" section should be read before treating any Tier 2 source casually.

### Tier 3 — Higher Risk

**Footage from sources with active enforcement histories or ambiguous rights.**

- Agency/stock-style footage that looks free but isn't clearly licensed.
- Content from creators who have shown enforcement inclination.
- Footage that is clearly a near-whole reuse of someone else's curated/compiled clip rather than raw footage.

**What Tier 3 buys:** Sometimes engagement, sometimes nothing but risk.

**What Tier 3 doesn't buy:** A good argument. Near-whole reuse of a curated clip is the weakest position.

### Tier 4 — Avoid (Or Use Only With Explicit Permission)

- Footage with visible watermarks from stock/wire services.
- Footage from sources whose terms explicitly prohibit this kind of reuse.
- Compilations scraped from other creators' channels (this is double-risk: you're clipping someone else's clip of someone else's footage).

**Rule of thumb:** If the source has a watermark, a "do not use" notice, or a clear statement that reuse is prohibited, do not build the channel on it.

## 3. What "Free to Find" Looks Like in Practice

Free footage is everywhere; the skill is in finding the right footage and knowing what you're looking at. Below are the practical hunting grounds, with notes on what each tends to yield.

### Social platforms (other creators' uploads)

- TikTok, YouTube, Instagram — enormous volume of war/news clips already uploaded by other creators.
- **Risk note:** Clipping another creator's clip (rather than the underlying raw footage) compounds risk. It is the weakest source position. Prefer to trace back to the underlying footage when possible.
- **Use case:** Discovery, not necessarily sourcing. When you see a clip that performs, trace it to its origin before making it your source.

### Wire services' free/public feeds

- AP, Reuters, and others publish some content publicly; some is genuinely free to access (and some looks free but isn't). Read the specific terms.
- Wire service footage is high quality and high engagement but historically aggressive on licensing. This is Tier 2/3 border depending on the specific content and terms.

### Broadcast/news outlet social feeds and websites

- Many outlets post clips to YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, and their own sites. Access is free. Rights are with the outlet.
- This is the most common source category for this kind of channel. Tier 2.

### Public government / military / public-affairs feeds

- Military public affairs, government agencies, and some international equivalents publish footage.
- Public domain status is jurisdiction-dependent and content-dependent. Treat as Tier 1 candidate, verify per source, don't assume.

### NGO / humanitarian / advocacy footage

- NGOs, human rights organizations, and advocacy groups sometimes release footage; terms vary.
- Check the specific release/license terms. Some is explicitly permissive; some is for awareness only.

### User-generated footage from conflict zones

- Raw footage uploaded by people on the ground — high volatility in quality, sourcing, and rights.
- Hard to verify provenance; rights are unclear. Generally Tier 3 unless you can trace it.

### Archive / library / public-domain collections

- Internet Archive, national archives, and similar — some genuinely public-domain material.
- Lower engagement per clip but cleaner rights position. Worth knowing about for variety and for building a Tier 1 buffer.

## 4. Daily Hunt Workflow

This is the repeatable daily (or per-batch) process for finding clips. Keep it lightweight — sourcing shouldn't become a full-time hunt for a zero-budget test batch.

### Step 1 — Maintain a short list of hunting grounds

Keep a running list (a notes file or spreadsheet) of 8–15 specific places you check, broken down by category:

- 3–5 social search queries (e.g., specific conflict + "news footage," "wire," "broadcast," specific outlet names).
- 2–3 wire/outlet social feeds you follow.
- 1–2 archive/library sources for Tier 1 material.
- 1–2 NGO/humanitarian sources if relevant to your coverage areas.

The list should be short enough to actually run daily and specific enough to be repeatable. "Search TikTok for war stuff" is not a hunting ground. "Search TikTok for [specific conflict] + 'footage' sorted by most liked in the last 7 days" is.

### Step 2 — Search with intent

When you search, search for **clip-able moments**, not just any footage:

- Sharable moments: a clear, self-contained event in a clip (an interview excerpt, a battlefield development, a press briefing moment, a statement).
- Clips with a natural headline: footage where the event is identifiable enough that you can write a specific headline rather than a vague one.
- Footage with enough surrounding context that you can add value via overlays (timestamps, context lines, what's happening, who's speaking).

Avoid footage that is just chaotic raw material with no identifiable event — it's hard to add transformation value to, and it tends to be the most sensitive-content-flagged material.

### Step 3 — Vet before clipping

For each candidate clip, run a quick vetting pass before you clip it:

1. **Source identification:** Who uploaded it? What's the underlying source if you can tell? Is it a watermark? Is it someone else's compilation?
2. **Tier assignment:** Assign a risk tier per Section 2. Don't clip Tier 4. Be deliberate about Tier 2.
3. **Clip-ability check:** Is there a clear, self-contained moment? Is the resolution/quality usable? **Pull a frame from the candidate segment and run vision_analyze on it. The source must be clear enough to watch — no heavily blurred, low-res, or unwatchable footage. If the frame is blurry/unwatchable, flag the candidate and find a better source or segment.** Is it long enough to clip from or too long (in which case you're picking a segment)?
4. **Source audio language:** Determine the language of the source audio from the outlet and clip context. **Log it explicitly.** If the source audio is not English and the clip does not carry English captions/subtitles, **flag to Leo before producing** — do not produce a clip with foreign-language audio and no English text track. The channel publishes in English; non-English audio requires either an English-language source or explicit Leo approval.
5. **Sensitive-content flag check:** Does the footage carry explicit graphic content that will trip platform flags regardless of copyright? (See Legal Report: "Graphic / sensitive content policies for war content.") This is a platform risk, not just a copyright risk, and it can be the binding constraint.

### Step 4 — Log it

Log every candidate you vet, even the ones you don't clip yet. A simple log entry:

- Source / URL
- Date found
- Tier (1/2/3)
- What the clip is (one line)
- Clip-ability notes
- Sensitive-content notes
- Decision: clip now / hold / pass

This log becomes your backlog. It also lets you spot patterns: which sources produce usable clips, which produce nothing but risk, which produce clips that keep getting flagged.

### Step 5 — Prefer variety

Don't build your entire feed from one source. Source variety is a risk-control pattern: it reduces reliance on any single rights-holder, and it reduces the chance that one enforcement action takes out most of your content. The Legal Report's "rotation of sources" pattern is the theory; this is the practice.

A minimal source log (spreadsheet or notes file). One row per candidate clip.

|-------|-------|

| Source name | Outlet / uploader / feed |
| Source URL | Where you found it |

| Sensitive-content flag | Explicit graphic content? Age-gate risk? |
| Overlay potential | Can you write a specific headline / context? |
| Decision | Clip now / hold / pass |
| Notes | Anything else worth remembering |

## 6. Zero-Budget Sourcing — Honest Limits

Be honest about what zero budget does and doesn't let you do:

**Zero budget lets you:**
- Access free footage from free sources.
- Build transformation overlays.
- Publish to platforms that have free accounts.
- Run a small test batch without spending money.

**Zero budget does NOT let you:**
- License anything that requires a license fee.
- Resolve ambiguous rights by paying for clearance.
- Buy your way out of a platform enforcement action.
- Access paid archival or licensed footage collections.

**The practical implication:** For a zero-budget channel in this category, the sourcing strategy is predominantly Tier 1 (where available) and selective Tier 2, with the transformation work (overlays, context, clip selection) doing the heavy lifting on the defensibility side. The Legal Report should be your companion on where the line is between a plausible fair-use argument and just infringement.

## 7. What Good Sourcing Looks Like (Test-Batch Scale)

For a small test batch (a handful of clips over 1–2 weeks), good sourcing looks like:

- A short, specific hunting-ground list you actually run.
- A vetted backlog of candidates before you start editing.
- Deliberate tier assignments, not accidental ones.
- Variety across sources (not all from one outlet/feed).
- A few Tier 1 candidates in the mix where you can find them, to build a cleaner-position buffer.
- Explicit sensitive-content vetting before you edit — the platform risk is real and can be the binding constraint.

## 8. Where Sourcing Meets the Rest of the Build

- **Overlays (`02-overlays.md`):** The overlay is your transformation work. Sourcing decides what raw material you have to transform; overlays decide how. A Tier 2 source with strong, specific overlays is a more defensible position than a Tier 2 source with sensationalist overlay text.
- **Editing (`03-editing-tooling.md`):** Your clip length and how much of the source you use are editing decisions that affect risk. Shorter, selected clips with added context are a stronger position than long near-whole reuses.
- **Distribution (`04-distribution.md`):** Platform-specific rules affect what you can publish from a given source. A clip that's fine on one platform may be flagged on another.
- **Risk register (`09-risk-register.md`):** Every sourcing choice maps to risk. The register tracks which build decisions raise or lower which risks.
