# Overlay Design — The Transformation Work

**This is where defensibility lives.** The same source footage is more or less defensible, more or less clickable, and more or less valuable depending on what you layer onto it. For a text-overlay-only, zero-budget channel, the overlay is the product.

---

## 1. What Overlays Actually Do

An overlay changes the relationship between the viewer and the footage:

- **Adds context** — who, what, when, where, why, what happens next
- **Adds framing** — what the viewer should pay attention to, what's at stake
- **Adds attribution** — where the footage came from (good practice, not a defense)
- **Adds editorial voice** — even text-only, the choice of words, the angle, the emphasis is editorial

A thin overlay restates what's visible. A strong overlay gives the viewer something the raw clip doesn't give them on its own.

---

## 2. The Clickable + Defensible Tension

The brief asks for "highly trending, highly clickable." That creates a real tension:

| Clickable often tempts toward | Defensible/valuable needs |
|-------------------------------|--------------------------|
| Sensational headline that overclaims | Accurate, specific headline that's still interesting |
| Vague hype ("You won't believe...") | Specific context that makes the clip worth watching |
| Overlay that's mostly noise | Overlay that adds information the viewer needs |
| Multiple competing text elements | Clean hierarchy: one main headline, supporting context |

The right move is **clickable-and-real**, not **clickable-by-any-means**. A clip that gets clicks from a misleading overlay and then disappoints the viewer is a bad channel decision — it erodes retention, trust, and long-term performance. A clip with a specific, interesting, accurate headline that makes the viewer want to see the footage is the better play on every dimension that matters.

---

## 3. Overlay Anatomy

A well-structured clip overlay has a hierarchy:

### Headline (the main line)

The single most important text element. Should be:
- **Specific** — names, places, events, stakes. "Ukraine strikes Russian airbase in Buryatia" beats "Big strike happens."
- **Interesting** — worth clicking. The specificity is what makes it interesting, not vagueness.
- **Accurate** — misrepresenting the clip is a trust and quality failure, and can also create its own problem if the clip/source doesn't support the claim.
- **Sized for the format** — readable at the platform's typical viewing size; not so long it crowds the frame.

### Context line(s) (supporting)

One to three lines that answer the questions the headline raises or that the raw footage doesn't make obvious:
- Who is involved / speaking
- What is happening / has happened
- What's at stake / what follows
- Timestamp or time reference when relevant

Keep context tight. If you need more than 2–3 lines, ask whether the clip is too thin for the overlay approach or whether you're over-explaining.

### Timestamps / time references

Useful when the clip relates to a time-sensitive development:
- When the event happened
- When the footage was published / aired
- "As of [date]" framing when relevant

### Source attribution

Small, consistent. Includes the source outlet/uploader where known. This is good practice — not a legal or platform shield, but honest and trust-supporting.

## 4. Overlay Quality Tiers

Treat overlay quality as something you can deliberately vary and measure, not just "done" vs. "not done."

### Tier A — Strong overlay

- Specific, accurate headline
- 1–3 lines of real added context
- Clean hierarchy, readable at platform size
- Source attributed
- The overlay genuinely adds something the clip doesn't say on its own
- The headline is clickable because it's specific and interesting, not because it's loud

### Tier B — Adequate overlay

- Headline is specific enough and accurate
- Some context added, but thinner
- Readable
- Source attributed
- The clip-plus-overlay is arguably better than the clip alone, but not strongly

### Tier C — Thin overlay

- Headline restates or vaguely references what's visible
- Little or no added context
- May be sensationalist without being informative
- The transformation posture is weak

### Tier D — Poor overlay (avoid)

- Misleading or overclaimed headline
- So much text it crowds the frame or competes with the footage
- No source attribution when source is known
- The overlay fails to add value and/or actively misleads

## 5. What Makes an Overlay Defensible (vs. Just a Text Layer)

A defensible overlay, in the fair-use sense, is one that supports a "transformative use" argument — i.e., the clip-plus-overlay is arguably a different work than the clip alone, with a different purpose or character.

Relevant overlay characteristics:
- **Adds commentary/context/criticism** — not just decoration. The overlay should contribute meaning.
- **Doesn't just restate** — a headline that says exactly what the clip already shows is thinner than one that adds the "why" or "what next."
- **Is substantial enough to matter** — a single timestamp over a wholesale clip is thinner than a headline + context + attribution that re-frames what the viewer is getting.
- **Doesn't turn the clip into a derivative that primarily replaces the original** — the overlay should add a layer, not just repackage the same content for the same purpose.

The honest framing: text-overlay-only, no voiceover, no commentary, on a wholesale clip is the thinner end of the transformative-use lever. That doesn't mean every such clip is infringing — it means the fair-use posture is weaker and less certain than it would be with voiceover, commentary, or substantial original analysis layered in. If the channel later adds voiceover or commentary, that changes the posture for those clips.

## 6. Overlay Design for Different Content Types

The overlay approach should reflect the content type. War footage, tech news, and viral trending clips each have different overlay needs.

### War / conflict footage

- **Accuracy is paramount** — misrepresenting conflict footage is a serious trust failure and can create real-world problems.
- **Context over sensationalism** — what's happening, who's involved, what's at stake, what's disputed. The clickable angle comes from specificity and stakes, not from hype.
- **Be explicit about uncertainty** — if something is reported but not confirmed, say so. "Reported" vs. "confirmed" matters.
- **Sensitive content screening** is a separate, prior step — see the build plan's distribution and test-batch files.

### Tech news

- **Specifics sell** — product names, specs, announcements, who said what, what changed. Tech audiences respond to detail.
- **Context: why it matters** — a product announcement is more clickable with a line about what it means, who it affects, what's new vs. iterative.
- **Cleaner source posture on some tech coverage** — product launches, official announcements, executive statements can have a different (sometimes cleaner) sourcing profile than wire service war footage. Not automatically cleaner — verify per source.

### Viral / highly trending / clickable news

- **Speed + accuracy** — trending means timing matters, but accuracy still matters. A viral clip that's wrong is a trust failure.
- **The click comes from the event** — identifiable, timely, consequential. The overlay should make the event legible, not manufacture a sensationalism engine around it.
- **Beware the reposting-read** — viral news is also the category where reused-content/compilation patterns can be most visible if the output looks like reposting others' viral clips with loud text. The transformation needs to be real.

## 7. Overlay Production Workflow

### Step 1 — Write to the source, not to the click

Before you write the headline, know what the clip actually shows and what the underlying event is. Write the headline from that, not from what you think will get the most clicks regardless of truth.

### Step 2 — Draft the headline

One line. Specific. Interesting. Accurate.

Test against: Would a viewer who reads only the headline and then watches the clip feel the headline was fair? Would a viewer who knows the underlying event feel misrepresented?

### Step 3 — Draft context lines

1–3 lines. Answer the questions the headline raises. Add what the clip doesn't say.

### Step 4 — Add time reference if relevant

Timestamps, "as of" framing, when the footage was published.

### Step 5 — Add source attribution

Small, consistent. Source outlet/uploader where known.

### Step 6 — Review for hierarchy and readability

Is the headline the most prominent? Is there too much text? Is it readable at the target platform's size? Is anything competing with the footage for attention?

### Step 7 — Log the overlay decisions

Part of the clip log: what headline you used, what context you added, what tier the overlay is (A/B/C/D), and any notes on accuracy or uncertainty.

## 8. Overlay Templates (Conceptual)

These are not copy-paste generators — they're structures to fill.

### Template 1 — Event/development clip

```
[HEADLINE: Specific event, who + what + where]
[CONTEXT: What happened / is happening, stakes, what's next]
[TIMESTAMP: When — event time, footage time if relevant]
[SOURCE: Outlet/uploader]
```

### Template 2 — Statement / quote clip

```
[HEADLINE: Who said what, in brief]
[CONTEXT: The remark's substance, the setting, why it matters, any dispute/controversy]
[TIMESTAMP: When said, when published]
[SOURCE: Who said it, where published]
```

### Template 3 — Product / tech announcement

```
[HEADLINE: What was announced / released / changed, by whom]
[CONTEXT: What's new, what it means, who it affects, what's not new]
[TIMESTAMP: Announcement date, if relevant]
[SOURCE: Company/outlet/person]
```

### Template 4 — Viral / trending news

```
[HEADLINE: The event, specific and timely]
[CONTEXT: What's happening, why it's spreading, any key nuance]
[TIMESTAMP: When it happened / went viral, if relevant]
[SOURCE: Where it originated / where you found it]
```

## 9. Where Overlay Design Meets the Rest of the Build

- **Sourcing (`01-sourcing.md`):** Sourcing decides the raw material. Overlay design decides how much value you can add. A clip with a clear, identifiable event and enough context to build on is a better overlay candidate than chaotic raw footage.
- **Editing (`03-editing-tooling.md`):** The overlay is rendered in the editing tool. Clip length and how much of the source you use are editing decisions that interact with the overlay's defensibility.
- **Distribution (`04-distribution-workflow.md`):** Overlay legibility and text density should be checked against each platform's typical viewing size and aspect ratio.
- **Test batch (`05-first-test-batch.md`):** The test batch should include deliberate overlay-quality variation so you can see whether stronger overlays correlate with better performance.
- **Risk register (`09-risk-register.md`):** Overlay quality maps to copyright/fair-use risk (thinner overlays = weaker posture) and to platform reused-content perception (more original, value-adding overlay = further from "just reposting").

---

## 10. Honest Limits

- **Overlays don't turn infringement into non-infringement.** A strong overlay improves the posture; it doesn't guarantee safety.
- **Overlays don't override platform sensitivity rules.** A well-overlaid war clip can still be removed for graphic content.
- **Overlays don't make a bad clip good.** If the underlying footage is weak, sensational, or problematic, a good overlay won't rescue it.
- **The clickable goal doesn't override accuracy.** A misleading clickable overlay is a bad channel decision.
